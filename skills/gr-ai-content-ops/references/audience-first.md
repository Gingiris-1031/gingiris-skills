# Audience-first editorial gate

## Find a reason to read before filling a calendar

For each topic, answer in plain language:
- Who encounters this situation, and at what moment?
- What are they trying to gain, avoid, understand or decide?
- What concrete observation, example or explanation will reward their attention?
- Why can this author/product contribute something credible here?

Find reader language in available customer questions, support/sales records, relevant public conversations or consented interviews. Preserve source and context internally. User-confirmed experience is valid first-party material; assist with specific checks. An inferred scenario can be written as an illustration, not a reported customer incident. If evidence is thin, write a useful small piece or ask for one missing example instead of padding a universal framework.

## Select an angle

Develop 2–3 different angles before writing: a costly misunderstanding, a concrete decision with tradeoffs, a revealing demonstration, an overlooked detail, or an earned lesson. These are options, not mandatory conflict/contrarian formulas. State the reader payoff for each and choose based on specificity, relevance and evidence. Do not invent urgency, losses, consensus or controversy.

A topic such as 'AI handoff best practices' is not yet an angle. 'The morning employee has a transcript but still cannot return the call' supplies a situation and a practical question, provided it is framed as an illustration unless sourced.

## Write for attention and reward it

Open with the actual situation, observation or consequential question. Advance the thought: show a concrete detail, explain why it changes a decision, explore the relevant tradeoff and deliver a useful next step. Choose the structure that suits the material; do not mechanically repeat scene-conflict-lesson for every post.

Replace generic terms with things readers recognise: a transcript, a missing address, a price question, a calendar entry, the colleague returning a call. Explain product value in the reader's work, without making unsupported capability claims. A checklist belongs where it helps the reader act, not as a substitute for reasoning. Remove repeated warnings, empty rhetorical questions and unnecessary brand mentions. First-person experience belongs only to the actual speaker.

Choose a visual that contains useful information or demonstrates the point. A diagram brief is not a finished asset. The final CTA should follow from the reader's problem; an educational post need not carry a sales link if it adds nothing.

## Review before delivery

Read the draft as the intended buyer, not as an SEO auditor:
1. Is the opening recognisably relevant, or could it introduce any SaaS product?
2. Does the reader learn something concrete beyond 'define goals, measure and iterate'?
3. Does each paragraph develop the point rather than repeat it?
4. Does the evidence support the conclusion and the author's perspective?
5. Would the content still be useful without its CTA?

If it fails, revise the angle/example before adjusting length or keywords. Editorial review is a proxy, not proof readers like it. Validate through real target-reader feedback and available saves, substantive replies, shares, qualified clicks or business actions. Record sample/window and distinguish reactions from buyer fit. Use findings to choose the next topic; do not promise virality from a rubric or fabricate reader-panel feedback.
